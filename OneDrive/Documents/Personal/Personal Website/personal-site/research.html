Research

Field research on how people learn, are assessed, and stay motivated at work.

My work spans academic, government, and military settings, and now continues at the University of Rhode Island. I use randomized experiments, longitudinal surveys, structural equation modeling, and multilevel models, and I work closely with the organizations involved so the findings inform real decisions.

Training & workplace learning

When learning happens on a screen and nobody is watching, what keeps attention on the task?

Learning is one of the most consequential things people do at work, and a growing share of it is self-paced and online. My work examines how attention, mindfulness, and self-regulation shape what people take away from training. It includes a 2×2 experiment in an air traffic control simulation that separates the effects of actual mindfulness practice from simply calling something “mindfulness,” and a conceptual model of mindful training that brings mindfulness theory together with established principles of learning science.

Takeaway

In self-directed online learning, off-task attention is especially costly. Strategies such as goal setting and mindfulness may help learners stay focused when distraction is the default.

Assessment & selection

How do we know a leadership assessment measures something real, and treats people fairly?

Organizations make decisions about selection, development, and promotion based on assessments. I build and evaluate those tools: defining what good performance looks like in a specific role, writing items that capture it, and testing whether scores are reliable, valid, and fair.

At the Naval Submarine Medical Research Laboratory I contributed to the Submarine Environment Fit (SUBFIT) assessment and to a leadership competency model for submarine command teams, along with a method for gathering criterion-related validity evidence for it. At the Center for the Army Profession and Leadership, I led the development and validation of leadership assessments that support Army leader development.

Takeaway

“Good leadership” looks different from one role to the next. Defining role-specific competencies first is what makes later selection and feedback tools defensible.

Key work

  • Leadership competency model for submarine command triads (2023), DTIC technical report
  • SUBFIT Assessment: Psychometric review (2022), DTIC technical report
  • Development of a refugee job search stressor scale (Academy of Management, 2021), from an NSF-funded project
03

Motivation & well-being

Can a good life outside work make up for needs that go unmet at work?

Self-determination theory holds that people need autonomy, competence, and relatedness. My dissertation, Barking up the Wrong Need, tested whether satisfying a need outside work that goes unmet at work produces better recovery. Using a time-lagged design and response surface analysis, I found that need satisfaction outside work predicted well-being and recovery overall, but it did not compensate for the specific needs that work left unmet.

I carried this line of work into two Defense Health Program grants as co-investigator, studying burnout, need satisfaction, and training success among submariners.

Takeaway

Need satisfaction at work and outside it are both necessary, not interchangeable. A great weekend won’t fully offset a job that leaves people without autonomy, competence, or connection.

Key work

  • Hanson, Randall, & Banfi, need satisfaction across life domains (manuscript under review)
  • How psychological need satisfaction and motivation influence burnout in submariners (Defense Health Program, $130K, co-investigator)
  • Submariner training success: Predicting graduation from the submarine training pipeline (Defense Health Program, $265K, co-investigator)

Next: AI-assisted assessment & feedback

Can conversational AI deliver developmental feedback that is both scalable and scientifically sound?

Good developmental feedback is expensive to deliver at scale. I’m exploring how large language models can be paired with validated assessment frameworks to administer assessments conversationally and generate personalized feedback, without giving up the psychometric standards that make the results trustworthy. I’m also interested in how these tools can help translate competency models into practical guidance, for example for service members moving into civilian careers.
