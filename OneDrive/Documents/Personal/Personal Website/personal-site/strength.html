Did more competition close the strength gap?

Women’s powerlifting has grown enormously over the past twenty years. I used 2.7 million competition results to ask whether women’s strength grew faster than men’s as the field expanded, and how much of today’s gap reflects a historically thinner pool of competitors.

Matthew D. Hanson · October 2026 · Code on GitHub

Key findings

  1. Women’s participation in drug-tested raw powerlifting grew about 281-fold from 2000 to 2024, versus 87-fold for men.
  2. The gap narrowed at every level. From 2012 to 2025, top-10 women went from 72% to 77% of men’s bodyweight-adjusted strength, and the median lifter from 63% to 68%.
  3. Field size explains part of it: roughly a quarter to a half of the elite narrowing disappears when women and men are compared in equally sized fields.
  4. The rest looks like real improvement. Holding constant who competes, a machine-learning model still shows women gaining on men, and women improve faster early in their careers.
  5. None of this suggests the gap is closing entirely. At the elite level women remain at about 75–78% of men under every method.

The question

Men outperform women in absolute strength, largely because of physiological differences that emerge at puberty. But performance at the top of any sport also depends on how many people compete. A bigger pool produces more exceptional performers, and for decades far fewer women than men competed in strength sports.

That raises a measurable question: as women’s participation caught up, did women’s performance grow faster than men’s, and how much of the remaining gap is about the size of the talent pool?

The data

I used the public OpenPowerlifting database (updated October 9, 2026), which records full-meet results (squat, bench press, and deadlift) from federations worldwide. After cleaning, that is 2.67 million results. The main analysis uses drug-tested, raw competition, with no supportive suits or bench shirts, which is the fairest like-for-like comparison. It covers 377,621 lifters.

Because men are heavier on average, I compare bodyweight-adjusted strength: a lifter’s total divided by bodyweight to the 2/3 power, a standard scaling from muscle physiology. Among the strongest lifters, this exponent fits both sexes almost exactly. Each lifter counts once per year, using their best meet.

The gap narrowed at every level

Women’s share of lifters rose from 14% in 2000 to 33% in 2024. Over the same period, women’s strength relative to men’s rose among the very best, at the 99th percentile, and for the typical lifter. Raw lifting became the main stage for elite competitors around 2012, so I treat 2012–2025 as the primary window. Earlier raw results are thinner and less representative.

How much is just a bigger pool?

The top of a larger field is higher simply by chance: the best of 20,000 people beats the best of 500 even when both groups come from the same population. To separate that statistical effect from real change, I compared women and men drawn from equally sized pools, repeatedly sampling 400 lifters of each sex per year.

Field size across countries

Across methods, roughly a quarter to a half of the elite narrowing since 2012 is attributable to the women’s field getting bigger. The rest persists even in equal-size comparisons.

A model across 91 countries (country fixed effects and separate year effects for each sex) points the same way. Elite performance rises with field size for both sexes, but about twice as steeply for women: each tenfold increase in field size goes with roughly 11% higher elite strength for women versus 6% for men. That fits a growing pool drawing in talented women who previously would never have competed. Field size isn’t randomly assigned, though, so this is an association, not proof of cause.

Women improve faster early on

Following 30,000 lifters who started between 2012 and 2020 and competed at least three times, a mixed-effects growth model shows women gaining strength proportionally faster in their first years. A likely reason is more room to grow, since many women arrive with less prior strength training.

Machine learning: is it just who’s competing?

As the sport grew, the mix of lifters changed: more novices, different ages and bodyweights, more countries. To check that the narrowing isn’t simply a change in who competes, I trained gradient-boosted tree models on 485,871 lifter-years and tested them on 121,208 results from lifters the models never saw.

  • A typical-performance model explains 69% of the variation in held-out lifters’ strength, versus 59% from sex, age, and bodyweight alone.
  • An elite model, trained to predict the 95th percentile, is well calibrated: 94.1% of held-out results fall below its predictions, against a 95% target.

I then took each sex’s actual lifters and changed only the calendar year, asking the models what the same people would be predicted to lift in 2012 versus 2025. The gap still narrows, from 67.7% to 70.8% for elite performance and from 64.7% to 68.0% for typical performance, so a changing mix of lifters doesn’t explain it away.

The longer view

Drug-tested equipped lifting has records back to the 1980s. There, the top-10 ratio rose from about 69% in 1990 to 79% in 2025, the same direction over a much longer span. The pattern also holds in untested federations and when wrist wraps are excluded.

What it means

The answer to the original question is yes, partly. Women’s strength has grown faster than men’s as competition expanded, and the historically smaller pool of women competitors did make the measured gap look larger than it otherwise would have. But field size accounts for only part of the narrowing, and even in equal comparisons a substantial gap remains. Most of today’s gap among elite lifters is not a participation artifact.

For people who study performance and talent, the broader lesson is about measurement: comparisons between groups are only fair when the groups had comparable opportunity to produce their best performers. That applies to selection systems and leadership pipelines as much as to sport.

Methods & limitations

Pipeline. Python (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn, SHAP), in five scripts: data preparation, exploration, statistical models, machine learning, and export. Code is on GitHub; the raw data is public from OpenPowerlifting. An independent methods review checked the analysis, and two estimates that proved unstable were dropped.

Limitations. These are observational competition records, not samples of underlying ability. Raw drug-tested lifting grew alongside a shift away from equipped lifting, so early-year elites under-represent the strongest lifters. “Drug-tested” is a federation label, not individual testing. The 2/3 bodyweight scaling fits elite lifters of both sexes but changes the gap’s level, though not its trend. Field size is not randomly assigned, and within one country it rises almost in step with time, which limits how cleanly field depth can be separated from general improvement. Career analyses include only lifters who kept competing. Bootstrap intervals for extreme statistics are approximate. Machine-learning importance describes the model’s predictions, not causes. Estimates of the share due to field size vary by method and should be read as indicative.
